Prospects for adiponectin-based therapeutics in myocardial ischemia reperfusion injury.

ReviewPharmacol Ther · Bahit MC, Gibson CM, Lei Y et al. · Full text
Abstract

Adiponectin is an adipocyte-derived hormone with broad cardiometabolic benefits, circulating at high levels in healthy individuals but reduced in obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease. Low adiponectin concentrations are strongly associated with coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI), and heart failure. MIRI remains a major unmet need in contemporary ST-segment-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) care. Although reperfusion is essential in limiting ischemic myocardial necrosis, it causes deleterious effects such as impaired carbohydrate oxidation, excessive fatty-acid oxidation, intracellular acidosis, calcium (Ca2+) overload, oxidative/nitrative stress, and mitochondrial fragmentation, that expand infarct size, worsen microvascular obstruction, and fuel adverse ventricular remodeling. Activation of the adiponectin-adiponectin receptor (AdipoR) axis directly counteracts these processes by stimulating adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK)-dependent metabolic rebalancing, restoring mitochondrial fusion-fission homeostasis, enhancing oxidative capacity, reducing inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS-) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase-mediated injury, improving endothelial nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, and enhancing microvascular perfusion. In animal models, adiponectin or its mimetics consistently reduced infarct size, improved myocardial function, and attenuated apoptosis, inflammation, and no-reflow. A growing array of therapeutic strategies has emerged, each bringing its own pharmacologic nuances and implications for translation to the clinic. This review summarizes current strategies to enhance adiponectin action, highlights the progress in peptide and small-molecule mimetics, and discusses future directions for clinical translation in cardiometabolic diseases.

Smpd2 m6A modification contributes to hypoxic pulmonary hypertension via an alveolar epithelial cell senescence-associated phenotype.

New todayTissue Cell · Jiang J, Xu J, Wu T et al. · Full text
Abstract

BACKGROUND: Pulmonary vascular remodelling induced by the abnormal proliferation of pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells (PASMCs) is a core pathological feature of hypoxic pulmonary hypertension (HPH), but its exact pathogenesis remains poorly understood. This study aimed to elucidate the functional role and intrinsic regulatory mechanisms of an alveolar epithelial cell (AEC) senescence-associated phenotype in the pathogenesis of HPH. METHODS: A 4-week hypoxia-induced HPH rat model and an in vitro hypoxia-induced AEC senescence-associated phenotype model were established. The effects of hypoxia on AECs were analysed by evaluating the senescence-associated phenotype and mitochondrial function. In addition, a Transwell coculture system and AEC-conditioned medium were used to evaluate the effects of hypoxia-exposed AECs with a senescence-associated phenotype on PASMC proliferation. The key signalling pathways associated with the hypoxia-induced AEC senescence-associated phenotype were identified through transcriptome sequencing and subsequent KEGG pathway enrichment analysis. The roles of the Smpd2/ceramide axis in the hypoxia-induced AEC senescence-associated phenotype, PASMC proliferation and HPH progression were explored via the administration of METTL3- and Smpd2-targeting siRNAs, AAV6-shSmpd2 for lung-directed Smpd2 silencing in vivo, the neutral sphingomyelinase (nSMase) inhibitor GW4869, the mitochondria-targeted antioxidant Mito-TEMPO (MT) and the NAD⁺ precursor β-nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN). Finally, MeRIP-qPCR, RIP-qPCR, mRNA stability assays, and dual-luciferase assays were employed to elucidate the mechanisms of m6A RNA methylation in AECs with the senescence-associated phenotype. RESULTS: Chronic hypoxia induced typical manifestations of HPH in rats, accompanied by a more pronounced AEC senescence-associated phenotype. In cultured human AECs, hypoxia upregulated METTL3, which increased Smpd2 mRNA stability through m6A modification recognized by IGF2BP2. These changes were associated with ceramide accumulation and mitochondrial dysfunction and contributed to the AEC senescence-associated phenotype in vitro. AECs with this phenotype released soluble and/or vesicular paracrine signals that were associated with abnormal PASMC proliferation, contributing to vascular remodelling and right ventricular hypertrophy. In cultured human AECs, NMN pretreatment, Smpd2 knockdown, and METTL3 knockdown attenuated the hypoxia-induced AEC senescence-associated phenotype. In hypoxia-exposed rats, NMN supplementation, lung-directed Smpd2 silencing, and pharmacological nSMase inhibition with GW4869 attenuated the AEC senescence-associated phenotype and alleviated HPH progression. CONCLUSIONS: This study provides preclinical evidence that, in hypoxia-exposed rats and cultured human AECs/PASMCs, a hypoxia-induced AEC senescence-associated phenotype may contribute to PASMC proliferation and HPH development. Mechanistically, in cultured human AECs, hypoxia exposure increased METTL3-mediated m6A modification of Smpd2 mRNA, which increased its stability in an m6A- and IGF2BP2-dependent manner and upregulated Smpd2 expression. The resulting increase in ceramide accumulation was associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and the senescence-associated phenotype in AECs. In this preclinical setting, AECs with this phenotype may further contribute to PASMC proliferation and HPH development. The findings of this study provide preclinical data supporting a candidate mechanism in HPH pathogenesis and identify candidate targets for future validation in human PH and cell-specific in vivo models.

Integrative genetic analyses for drug repurposing identify emodin as a candidate modulator of STAT3 signaling in abdominal aortic aneurysm.

Vascul Pharmacol · Liu Y, Xu Z, Deng L et al. · Full text
Abstract

Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is a life-threatening vascular disease lacking effective pharmacological therapies. To identify potential therapeutic targets and candidate compounds, we performed integrative genetic analyses for drug repurposing by combining Mendelian randomization (MR), summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR), and Bayesian colocalization. This framework prioritized multiple druggable candidates, among which STAT3 emerged as a key regulatory node. Subsequent functional studies demonstrated that emodin suppressed vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation, migration, phenotypic switching, and inflammatory responses. These effects were accompanied by reduced STAT3 phosphorylation and nuclear localization. Molecular docking suggested a potential binding mode of emodin within the STAT3 SH2 domain, while cellular thermal shift assays demonstrated thermal stabilization of STAT3 by emodin in intact cells, consistent with target engagement. Notably, emodin and the selective STAT3 inhibitor STAT3-IN-13 produced comparable effects, and co-treatment did not yield additive efficacy, suggesting overlapping mechanisms. In vivo, emodin attenuated aneurysm development and reduced vascular inflammation in experimental models. Collectively, these findings identify emodin as a candidate modulator of AAA progression acting through suppression of STAT3-associated signaling pathways, and demonstrate the potential of genetic evidence-guided drug repurposing strategies in complex vascular diseases.

New todayReviewFront Pharmacol · Fu X, Dai J, Wang X et al. · Full text
Abstract

Dickkopf-3 (DKK3) is a secreted glycoprotein that functions as a context-dependent modulator of the Wnt/β-catenin and transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) signaling pathways. Originally studied in development and cancer, DKK3 has recently emerged as a key stress-responsive factor. Finally, we explore the therapeutic implications of targeting the DKK3 axis, discuss the challenges posed by its context-dependent dual roles, and outline future research directions necessary to translate these discoveries into clinical applications for cardiovascular patients.

COL10A1+ fibroblasts define rupture-prone abdominal aortic aneurysms and enable molecular risk stratification.

J Transl Med · Zhuo H, Ou C, Duan M et al. · Full text
Abstract

BACKGROUND: Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) rupture remains the leading cause of AAA-related mortality, while current clinical management relies primarily on aneurysm diameter, which inadequately predicts rupture risk. Increasing evidence suggests that stromal cell heterogeneity critically shapes vascular remodeling; however, the specific fibroblast states associated with AAA rupture predisposition remain poorly defined. METHODS: We integrated cross-species single-cell RNA-sequencing datasets spanning the full spectrum of AAA progression to systematically characterize fibroblast heterogeneity and state transitions. Pseudotime trajectory analysis, transcriptional regulatory network inference, and ligand-receptor interaction modeling were applied to delineate fibroblast-associated microenvironmental programs. RESULTS: Single-cell analysis revealed a progressive transition from homeostatic IGFBP6+ fibroblasts to a disease-associated COL10A1+ fibroblast state during AAA progression. Translationally, leveraging this fibroblast state enabled the development of a minimal two-gene risk score (COL10A1-IGFBP6), which stratified AAA patients by molecular rupture risk independently of aneurysm diameter in the validation cohort. CONCLUSIONS: This study identifies a COL10A1+ fibroblast-associated transcriptional state that defines a rupture-prone vascular microenvironment in abdominal aortic aneurysm. The derived COL10A1-IGFBP6 signature establishes a biologically informed framework for molecular risk stratification, complementing anatomical size-based assessment and providing a foundation for future development of mechanism-guided strategies to refine AAA risk evaluation.

Retroperitoneal Hematoma in Essential Thrombocythaemia: A Diagnostic Challenge.

New todayCase reportClin Ter · Di Gennaro L, Foti F, Benedetti B et al. · Full text
Abstract

BACKGROUND: Essential thrombocythaemia (ET) is a chronic myeloproliferative neoplasm characterized by sustained thrombocytosis and an increased risk of both thrombotic and haemorrhagic events. While thrombotic complications are more frequent, major bleeding remains a rare but potentially fatal occurrence, particularly when anticoagulant therapy is required. Retroperitoneal haematoma represents one of the most severe bleeding manifestations, often linked to anticoagulation or vascular fragility. CASE REPORT: We report the case of an 85-year-old woman with ET, obesity, and hypertension who underwent elective total right hip replacement. Postoperatively, she developed pulmonary micro-embolism and was treated with high-dose enoxaparin. Four days later, she suffered sudden haemodynamic collapse due to a massive retroperitoneal haematoma. Enoxaparin was discontinued and replaced with tranexamic acid infusion. Despite urgent surgical evacuation, multiple transfusions, and intensive care, her condition worsened with the development of femoral vein thrombosis, multidrug-resistant sepsis, worsening pulmonary embolism, and severe thrombocytopenia. She died 34 days after surgery. Autopsy confirmed a large organized haematoma compressing the right renal lodge and inferior vena cava, associated with femoral vein thrombosis. DISCUSSION: This case highlights the therapeutic and diagnostic challenges of ET, in which thrombotic and haemorrhagic risks may coexist and be further complicated by antithrombotic therapy. Despite individualized enoxaparin dosing based on body weight, the patient developed a major retroperitoneal haemorrhage followed by femoral vein thrombosis. Although disseminated intravascular coagulation and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia were both considered in the differential diagnosis, insufficient clinical and laboratory evidence prevented definitive diagnostic confirmation of either condition. CONCLUSIONS: Early and accurate diagnostic assessment is crucial in ET patients, where thrombotic and haemorrhagic risks coexist. In the absence of clear guidelines, clinical judgment prevails, underscoring the need to reassess physicians' criminal liability.

Abstract

Cancer-associated venous thromboembolism is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in patients with solid tumors and is driven, in part, by tumor-induced metabolic reprogramming. The tryptophan (Trp)-kynurenine (Kyn) pathway, regulated by indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1), is frequently upregulated in cancer and generates bioactive metabolites with immunomodulatory and vascular effects. Although pharmacologic inhibition of IDO1 reduces thrombosis in tumor-bearing mice, the endothelial-specific contribution of IDO1 to cancer-associated thrombogenesis has not been genetically defined. Abstract

PURPOSE: To evaluate technical success, periprocedural safety and retrieval outcomes of inferior vena cava filter (IVCF) placement in adolescents with venous thromboembolism and to determine whether retrieval failure is associated with skeletal maturity. MATERIALS AND METHODS: Single-center retrospective review of 26 IVCF placed in 25 patients with iliocaval or iliofemoral thrombosis (mean age 15.8 ± 2.2 years; range 9-18) between 2006 and 2024. Skeletal maturity was graded by Risser stage. Retrieval was attempted once the initial indication had resolved and anticoagulation was feasible. RESULTS: Implantation succeeded in every case; mean caval diameter was 17.9 ± 3.0 mm. Inferior vena cava filter-related thrombosis: Mechanisms, clinical features, prevention, diagnosis, and risk-stratified management.

ReviewThromb Res · Xu W, Yu W, Zhu Y et al. · Full text
Abstract

Inferior vena cava (IVC) filters can provide mechanical protection from pulmonary embolism in selected patients who cannot receive anticoagulation, but filter-related thrombosis is an important complication. A uniform definition is lacking. Thrombus in the filter region may represent emboli captured by the filter, extension of pre-existing deep vein thrombosis, or local thrombus formation around the device. Filter-induced flow disturbance and vessel-wall responses may contribute to local thrombosis, although direct mechanistic evidence in humans remains limited. Reported occurrence varies substantially because study populations, imaging schedules, and outcome definitions differ. Clinically detected caval thrombosis or thrombotic occlusion is uncommon in large databases and prospective cohorts. Planned imaging before retrieval, however, often detects small intrafilter thrombi, most of which are asymptomatic. Accordingly, the clinical importance of an imaging-detected thrombus depends on whether it is accompanied by meaningful caval obstruction or symptoms. Prevention should continue throughout the filter life cycle. Filters should be used only for a time-limited, evidence-based indication. Anticoagulation should be resumed when a separate indication persists and bleeding risk permits, and retrieval should be assessed once filter-mediated pulmonary embolism protection is no longer required. Structured follow-up can help prevent loss to care. Management of established thrombosis should be guided by symptoms, impaired caval outflow, thrombus progression and chronicity, bleeding risk, and the need for filter retrieval. Anticoagulation remains foundational when indicated for active venous thromboembolism. Catheter-directed thrombolysis, mechanical thrombectomy, or combined treatment may be considered for selected symptomatic, progressive, or obstructive cases, but most supporting studies are nonrandomized. Future studies require standardized minimum reporting and patient-centered outcomes.
