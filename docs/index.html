Packing-accelerated consecutive NIR excitation (PACE) enables high energy photocatalysis and antitumor immunity in vivo.

Sci Adv · Liu Y, Wang S, Hu W et al. · Full text
Abstract

Near-infrared (NIR) photocatalysis promises deep-tissue compatibilities but remains fundamentally constrained by two bottlenecks: insufficient photon energy and rapid charge recombination. Here, we report packing-accelerated consecutive excitation (PACE), a mechanism enabling low energy near-infrared (NIR) photons to drive high energy single electron transfer (SET) reactions. We design a self-assembling ruthenium complex, Ru-3, that forms densely packed nanoaggregates exhibiting aggregation-stabilized excited states, a pronounced excited state absorption band at ∼1315 nm, and nanosecond scale triplet lifetimes. Under dual NIR-I/IIa irradiation (808 nm/1310 nm), Ru-3 nanoparticles exhibit photophysical behavior consistent with consecutive photon harvesting and access to a higher-energy reactive state that supports efficient SET catalysis under aqueous conditions. This PACE-driven reactivity enables robust oxidation of mitochondrial NADH to NAD+, disrupting electron-transport homeostasis, inducing cGAS-STING dependent pyroptosis, and reprogramming tumor-associated macrophages toward an M1 phenotype. In multiple murine tumor models, PACE-mediated photocatalysis elicits strong antitumor immunity and leads to tumor regression with minimal systemic toxicity. This work establishes PACE as a new photochemical paradigm that overcomes the intrinsic energy and charge-separation limits of NIR photoredox chemistry, opening a route toward high-energy photocatalysis in complex biological environments.

Maternal exposure to microplastics and nanoplastics: Placental lipid metabolic dysregulation as a potential link to fetal growth restriction.

ReviewReprod Toxicol · Gong W, Luo X, Wang H et al. · Full text
Abstract

Microplastics and nanoplastics (MNPs) are pervasive environmental contaminants linked to reproductive and developmental health. Plastic particles or polymeric residues have been detected in human blood, placenta, meconium, breast milk, fetal appendages, and umbilical venous blood, indicating maternal-fetal interface exposure during pregnancy. Fetal growth restriction (FGR) is a placental syndrome in which the fetus fails to achieve its growth potential, and impaired placental vascular, transport, endocrine, immune, and metabolic functions contribute to its pathogenesis. Experiments with polystyrene or polyethylene particles report fetal growth impairment, altered placental hemodynamics, defective trophoblast differentiation, mitochondrial and endoplasmic-reticulum stress, disrupted cholesterol and nicotinamide metabolism, lipid remodeling, and ferroptosis-associated injury. This targeted narrative review synthesizes human biomonitoring, animal, ex vivo, and omics evidence, with emphasis on placental fatty-acid uptake and oxidation, cholesterol trafficking, glycerophospholipid remodeling, nicotinamide/NAD⁺ homeostasis, oxidative lipid injury, and ferroptosis. Current evidence supports a plausible framework in which MNP exposure contributes to placental lipid metabolic dysregulation and thereby increases vulnerability to fetal growth impairment. However, human causal evidence remains insufficient, and the term MNPs should not obscure important differences between microplastics and nanoplastics in particle size, tissue penetration, detectability, and biological activity. Major uncertainties include contamination control, polymer and particle heterogeneity, environmental dose translation, gestational timing, co-exposure to additives and pollutants, and inconsistent FGR phenotyping. Prospective pregnancy cohorts and human-relevant placental models integrating validated particle detection, fetal growth trajectories, spatial and single-cell omics, metabolic-flux analysis, and rescue experiments are required to determine whether placental lipid dysregulation is a causal mediator of MNP-associated fetal growth restriction.

Mechanometabolic endothelial senescence as a translational framework for cardiometabolic vascular disease and vascular ageing.

ReviewMol Biol Rep · Lanlan L, Fan G, Ran Z et al. · Full text
Abstract

Cardiometabolic vascular disease (CMVD) is used in this Review as an operational umbrella for vascular injury arising from interacting metabolic, renal and cardiovascular stress, rather than as a universally standardised diagnosis. We examine mechanometabolic endothelial senescence as a framework linking local haemodynamic vulnerability with systemic cardiometabolic load. Evidence from human vascular tissue, clinical vascular-function studies, animal models, flow-controlled systems and endothelial-cell experiments indicates that low or oscillatory shear, matrix stiffness, hyperglycaemia, dyslipidaemia, uraemic and hepatic stress, inflammation and oxidative injury can converge on DNA-damage responses, p53/p21 and p16/pRB signalling, NAD⁺-sirtuin decline, mitochondrial dysfunction, metabolic rewiring and chromatin regulation. We therefore propose a staged validation strategy combining endothelial identity, convergent senescence markers, circulating-cell or extracellular-vesicle measures, vascular function and organ-specific outcomes. Mechanometabolic endothelial senescence is best viewed as a context-dependent disease amplifier and testable translational model, not a universal primary cause.

Mitochondrial dysfunction of renal tubular epithelial cells in chronic kidney disease-associated fibrosis: from organelle homeostasis to multicellular remodeling.

ReviewMol Biol Rep · Ni S, Li Z, Pan X et al. · Full text
Abstract

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a major global health burden, and renal fibrosis is a common pathological correlate of progressive loss of kidney function. Renal tubular epithelial cells (RTECs), particularly proximal tubular epithelial cells (PTECs), contain abundant mitochondria and depend on oxidative metabolism to support solute transport. This metabolic specialization renders them vulnerable to hypoxia, lipotoxicity, uremic toxins, aging, inflammation, and hemodynamic stress. Novel applications of SIRT1 in Chronic Kidney Disease.

ReviewFront Med (Lausanne) · Han Y, Wang Q, Pan X et al. · Full text
Abstract

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) has become a global public health issue, with progressive kidney fibrosis serving as a key pathological process leading to kidney failure, and current therapeutic approaches remain largely ineffective in reversing this progression. SIRT1, as an NAD⁺-dependent deacetylase, plays a central protective role in counteracting kidney fibrosis, inflammation, oxidative stress, and autophagic dysfunction by regulating multiple signaling pathways, and has emerged as a promising target in CKD treatment. This review systematically elaborates on the mechanisms regulating SIRT1 expression and activity in CKD, its protective roles in core pathological processes, and summarizes recent advances in targeting SIRT1 for CKD therapy. Functionally, SIRT1 antagonizes kidney fibrosis by deacetylating Smad3/Smad4 and inhibiting epithelial-mesenchymal transition; curbs inflammatory cascades through dual blockade of NF-κB transcriptional activity and NLRP3 inflammasome assembly; constructs a multi-layered antioxidant defense network by activating the Nrf2/ARE pathway, regulating FOXO transcription factors, and enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis; and restores autophagic flux to clear damaged organelles. Regarding complications, SIRT1 inhibits vascular calcification and improves kidney anemia by stabilizing HIF-1α to promote EPO synthesis and inhibiting ferroptosis. In terms of therapeutic strategies, the synthetic SIRT1 agonist SRT2104 has completed Phase I-II clinical trials with favorable safety profiles, but its pharmacokinetic limitations and the disconnect from preclinical effects pose barriers to clinical translation; NAD⁺ precursor strategies offer theoretical advantages but lack clinical evidence in CKD populations. In summary, SIRT1 integrates metabolic sensing, transcriptional regulation, and post-translational modification functions, serving as a key molecular hub linking upstream pathological signals to downstream organ damage in CKD. Precise modulation of SIRT1 activity may offer a novel multi-target synergistic therapeutic strategy for CKD.

Abstract

Isolation and cultivation of primary aortic smooth muscle cells (SMCs) provide a valuable approach for investigating cellular mechanisms underlying vascular diseases, including aortic aneurysms. Mouse Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm Model Induced by Perivascular Sheath Microinjection of Chymotrypsin.

New todayJ Vis Exp · Shen L, Zeng J, Huang R et al. · Full text
Abstract

Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is a life-threatening degenerative vascular disease for which no effective pharmacological therapy currently exists. Reliable and technically accessible animal models are essential for mechanistic and preclinical investigations. However, existing murine platforms are limited by technical complexity, prolonged operative time, or incomplete recapitulation of human AAA pathology. Here, this study presents a novel murine AAA model established by direct perivascular sheath microinjection of chymotrypsin. After midline laparotomy and identification of the infrarenal abdominal aorta, chymotrypsin working solution is injected directly into the perivascular sheath space under direct vision using a microscale needle. To determine the optimal enzyme concentration, a pilot dose-finding study was conducted using 0.125% (w/v; 1.25 mg/mL), 0.25% (w/v; 2.50 mg/mL), and 0.375% (w/v; 3.75 mg/mL) chymotrypsin (n = 5 per group), with PBS as negative control and topical PPE as positive control. Based on aneurysm incidence, aortic diameter progression, and mortality, 0.25% chymotrypsin was selected for the main study. The chymotrypsin group was directly compared with the PPE positive control group to benchmark the pathological features of this novel model. Serial Doppler ultrasonography confirmed a 100% aneurysm induction rate at day 14 in chymotrypsin-treated mice, whereas no dilation occurred in PBS-injected controls. Histological analyses revealed pronounced medial thickening, elastic fiber fragmentation, and collagen deposition, together with markedly elevated immunohistochemical expression of matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-2 and MMP-9 in aortic sections. Quantitative morphometry demonstrated significant increases in aortic wall thickness and collagen volume fraction, and a corresponding reduction in elastic fiber content in chymotrypsin-treated animals compared with controls. This protocol thus provides a technically streamlined, highly reproducible, and translationally relevant murine AAA model. Because the procedure avoids aortic dissection, arteriotomy, or specialized microsurgical training, it is well suited to laboratories investigating aneurysm pathobiology or evaluating novel pharmacological interventions.
