Prospects for adiponectin-based therapeutics in myocardial ischemia reperfusion injury.

ReviewPharmacol Ther · Bahit MC, Gibson CM, Lei Y et al. · Full text
Abstract

Adiponectin is an adipocyte-derived hormone with broad cardiometabolic benefits, circulating at high levels in healthy individuals but reduced in obesity, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease. Low adiponectin concentrations are strongly associated with coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, myocardial ischemia-reperfusion injury (MIRI), and heart failure. MIRI remains a major unmet need in contemporary ST-segment-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) care. Although reperfusion is essential in limiting ischemic myocardial necrosis, it causes deleterious effects such as impaired carbohydrate oxidation, excessive fatty-acid oxidation, intracellular acidosis, calcium (Ca2+) overload, oxidative/nitrative stress, and mitochondrial fragmentation, that expand infarct size, worsen microvascular obstruction, and fuel adverse ventricular remodeling. Activation of the adiponectin-adiponectin receptor (AdipoR) axis directly counteracts these processes by stimulating adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK)-dependent metabolic rebalancing, restoring mitochondrial fusion-fission homeostasis, enhancing oxidative capacity, reducing inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS-) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase-mediated injury, improving endothelial nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability, and enhancing microvascular perfusion. In animal models, adiponectin or its mimetics consistently reduced infarct size, improved myocardial function, and attenuated apoptosis, inflammation, and no-reflow. A growing array of therapeutic strategies has emerged, each bringing its own pharmacologic nuances and implications for translation to the clinic. This review summarizes current strategies to enhance adiponectin action, highlights the progress in peptide and small-molecule mimetics, and discusses future directions for clinical translation in cardiometabolic diseases.

Integrative genetic analyses for drug repurposing identify emodin as a candidate modulator of STAT3 signaling in abdominal aortic aneurysm.

Vascul Pharmacol · Liu Y, Xu Z, Deng L et al. · Full text
Abstract

Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) is a life-threatening vascular disease lacking effective pharmacological therapies. To identify potential therapeutic targets and candidate compounds, we performed integrative genetic analyses for drug repurposing by combining Mendelian randomization (MR), summary-data-based Mendelian randomization (SMR), and Bayesian colocalization. This framework prioritized multiple druggable candidates, among which STAT3 emerged as a key regulatory node. Subsequent functional studies demonstrated that emodin suppressed vascular smooth muscle cell proliferation, migration, phenotypic switching, and inflammatory responses. These effects were accompanied by reduced STAT3 phosphorylation and nuclear localization. Molecular docking suggested a potential binding mode of emodin within the STAT3 SH2 domain, while cellular thermal shift assays demonstrated thermal stabilization of STAT3 by emodin in intact cells, consistent with target engagement. Notably, emodin and the selective STAT3 inhibitor STAT3-IN-13 produced comparable effects, and co-treatment did not yield additive efficacy, suggesting overlapping mechanisms. In vivo, emodin attenuated aneurysm development and reduced vascular inflammation in experimental models. Collectively, these findings identify emodin as a candidate modulator of AAA progression acting through suppression of STAT3-associated signaling pathways, and demonstrate the potential of genetic evidence-guided drug repurposing strategies in complex vascular diseases.

Matrix metalloproteinase imaging and survival prospects with aging in murine aortic aneurysm.

EJNMMI Res · Ghim M, Varli O, Ahmad AA et al. · Full text
Abstract

BACKGROUND: Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) are elevated in aortic aneurysm (AA), where older age is a risk factor. The relationship among aging, aortic MMP activity, and AA development has not been systematically evaluated. This study leveraged MMP-targeted molecular imaging to investigate how aging affects aortic MMP activity, aneurysm development, and survival. RESULTS: AA development and animal survival were monitored for 28 days after Angiotensin (Ang)-II infusion in 8-10-week-old (young) and > 51-week-old (old) apolipoprotein E (Apoe)-/- mice. Old animals' survival at 28 days was significantly lower than that of young Ang-II-infused Apoe-/- mice (P < 0.05). 64Cu-RYM2 PET/CT showed significantly higher aortic MMP activation before and 1 week after Ang-II infusion in old compared to young Apoe-/- mice. The 64Cu-RYM2 signal was significantly higher in animals that did not survive 28 days than in those that did (P < 0.01). MMP activity significantly increased after Ang-II infusion and correlated with aortic macrophage infiltration. The increase in MMP activity preceded aortic dissection. Finally, MMP activity was heterogeneous along the aorta of control and Ang-II-infused young and old Apoe-/- mice, and of old wild-type mice. CONCLUSIONS: Aging is associated with increased MMP activity along the aorta and worse AA survival. MMP-targeted molecular imaging provides insights regarding aneurysm survival prospects in this exploratory cohort. Combining MMP inhibitors and tracers may help prevent and track aneurysm growth, dissection, and rupture.

J Transl Med · Zhuo H, Ou C, Duan M et al. · Full text
Abstract

BACKGROUND: Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) rupture remains the leading cause of AAA-related mortality, while current clinical management relies primarily on aneurysm diameter, which inadequately predicts rupture risk. Increasing evidence suggests that stromal cell heterogeneity critically shapes vascular remodeling; however, the specific fibroblast states associated with AAA rupture predisposition remain poorly defined. Endothelial indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 drives high-tryptophan diet-mediated thrombosis in cancer-bearing mice.

Blood Vessel Thromb Hemost · Jose A, Lotfollahzadeh S, Natrakul A et al. · Full text
Abstract

Cancer-associated venous thromboembolism is a major cause of morbidity and mortality in patients with solid tumors and is driven, in part, by tumor-induced metabolic reprogramming. The tryptophan (Trp)-kynurenine (Kyn) pathway, regulated by indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1), is frequently upregulated in cancer and generates bioactive metabolites with immunomodulatory and vascular effects. Although pharmacologic inhibition of IDO1 reduces thrombosis in tumor-bearing mice, the endothelial-specific contribution of IDO1 to cancer-associated thrombogenesis has not been genetically defined. Here, we used IDO1 global knockout and inducible IDO1 endothelial-specific knockout mice in a syngeneic MC38 tumor model combined with dietary Trp modulation to delineate the vascular role of IDO1. This suggested a selective amplification of procoagulant signaling by the Trp-IDO1-Kyn axis. Collectively, these findings provide direct genetic evidence that endothelial IDO1 is a contributing metabolic driver of cancer-associated thrombosis. Targeted control of the endothelial Trp-IDO1-Kyn pathway may represent a mechanistically informed strategy to mitigate thrombotic risk in patients with cancer.

Inferior vena cava filter-related thrombosis: Mechanisms, clinical features, prevention, diagnosis, and risk-stratified management.

New todayReviewThromb Res · Xu W, Yu W, Zhu Y et al. · Full text
Abstract

Inferior vena cava (IVC) filters can provide mechanical protection from pulmonary embolism in selected patients who cannot receive anticoagulation, but filter-related thrombosis is an important complication. A uniform definition is lacking. Thrombus in the filter region may represent emboli captured by the filter, extension of pre-existing deep vein thrombosis, or local thrombus formation around the device. Filter-induced flow disturbance and vessel-wall responses may contribute to local thrombosis, although direct mechanistic evidence in humans remains limited. Reported occurrence varies substantially because study populations, imaging schedules, and outcome definitions differ. Clinically detected caval thrombosis or thrombotic occlusion is uncommon in large databases and prospective cohorts. Planned imaging before retrieval, however, often detects small intrafilter thrombi, most of which are asymptomatic. Accordingly, the clinical importance of an imaging-detected thrombus depends on whether it is accompanied by meaningful caval obstruction or symptoms. Prevention should continue throughout the filter life cycle. Filters should be used only for a time-limited, evidence-based indication. Anticoagulation should be resumed when a separate indication persists and bleeding risk permits, and retrieval should be assessed once filter-mediated pulmonary embolism protection is no longer required. Structured follow-up can help prevent loss to care. Management of established thrombosis should be guided by symptoms, impaired caval outflow, thrombus progression and chronicity, bleeding risk, and the need for filter retrieval. Anticoagulation remains foundational when indicated for active venous thromboembolism. Catheter-directed thrombolysis, mechanical thrombectomy, or combined treatment may be considered for selected symptomatic, progressive, or obstructive cases, but most supporting studies are nonrandomized. Future studies require standardized minimum reporting and patient-centered outcomes.

Association of admission aggregate index of systemic inflammation with lower-extremity deep vein thrombosis after severe trauma.

PLoS One · Sun Q, Bian D · Full text
Abstract

Early identification of patients at risk for lower-extremity deep vein thrombosis (LEDVT) after severe trauma remains difficult. This retrospective cohort study evaluated whether admission aggregate index of systemic inflammation (AISI), derived from platelet, neutrophil, monocyte, and lymphocyte counts, was associated with ultrasound-confirmed in-hospital LEDVT detected before the earlier of discharge or post-injury day 30 and whether it improved risk stratification. Aspiration Thrombectomy for High-Risk Pulmonary Embolism With Life-Threatening Anemia and Active Uterine Bleeding: A Case Report.

Case reportAm J Case Rep · Saoudi O, Wang B · Full text
Abstract

BACKGROUND High-risk pulmonary embolism (PE) carries significant mortality, and management becomes complex when it coexists with severe anemia and active bleeding, as thrombolysis is then contraindicated. We report the case of a patient with high-risk PE complicated by severe anemia (hemoglobin 41 g/L) and active uterine bleeding from adenomyosis. CASE REPORT A 48-year-old woman presented with haemodynamic instability (blood pressure 74/47 mm Hg), critical anemia (hemoglobin 41 g/L), and a 3-year history of irregular vaginal bleeding from adenomyosis. Computed tomography (CT) pulmonary angiography confirmed bilateral PE with right pulmonary artery main-trunk occlusion; echocardiography showed severe pulmonary hypertension, and duplex ultrasound showed extensive left lower-limb deep vein thrombosis. Thrombolysis was contraindicated. Emergency percutaneous aspiration thrombectomy was performed using large-bore catheters (22F, 20F, 16F) with concurrent inferior vena cava filter placement, reducing pulmonary artery pressure from 51/23 to 34/12 mm Hg; the estimated blood loss, quantified from filtered aspirate, was approximately 100 mL. Adjuncts included transfusion of 13 units of packed red cells, dilatation and curettage, gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist therapy, and titrated anticoagulation. At discharge after a 24-day hospitalization she was hemodynamically stable, and free of dyspnea, with resolved edema and hemoglobin 72 g/L, on oral rivaroxaban. CONCLUSIONS In this thrombolysis-contraindicated patient, aspiration thrombectomy with inferior vena cava filter placement was a feasible rescue strategy that achieved rapid hemodynamic and angiographic improvement without thrombolysis, with palliative rather than curative intent. As a single case, it cannot establish comparative safety or efficacy, and broader conclusions regarding efficacy cannot be drawn. It illustrates a potentially useful approach in similarly complex scenarios where coordinated multidisciplinary care is essential.

Effect of Extended-Duration Venous Thromboprophylaxis After Cancer Surgery.

Ann Surg Oncol · Wang X, Chen W, Ma L et al. · Full text
Abstract

BACKGROUND: Cancer patients undergoing surgery are at significantly increased risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE). Although extended thromboprophylaxis is recommended, the optimal anticoagulant type and duration remain unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the comparative efficacy and safety of different extended thromboprophylaxis in this population. METHODS: A Bayesian network meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) was performed to evaluate various thromboprophylaxis strategies for patients undergoing cancer-related surgery. We conducted both three-node and five-node network analyses to evaluate extended-duration versus standard-duration thromboprophylaxis strategies. Outcomes included venous thromboembolism, major bleeding, and all-cause mortality. Net clinical benefit and risk-benefit assessments were additionally performed to determine the overall impact of prolonged anticoagulant therapy. CONCLUSIONS: Extended thromboprophylaxis is effective and safe for cancer patients undergoing surgery. Besides, DOACs appears to be the optimal anticoagulant for extended thromboprophylaxis, providing the greatest net clinical benefit.
